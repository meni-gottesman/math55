1Probability basics

  • Sample space S: every possible outcome. Event E: a set of outcomes, E ⊆ S.
  • Distribution: each outcome s gets p(s) ≥ 0, and they all add to 1.
p(E) = sum of p(s) over s in E
  • Uniform (all n outcomes equally likely): p(E) = |E| / |S|. Probability is counting with a denominator.
  • Not every die is fair. A die with p(6) = ½ and every other face 1/10 has p(even) = 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/2 = 7/10.
p(not E) = 1 − p(E)
p(E ∪ F) = p(E) + p(F) − p(E ∩ F)

Union bound: p(E₁ ∪ … ∪ Eₙ) ≤ p(E₁) + … + p(Eₙ). Always true, even when the events overlap.

2Conditional probability

p(E | F) = p(E ∩ F) / p(F)

Read it as “E given F.” Knowing F happened shrinks the world down to F.

Example

Flip two fair coins. p(both heads | at least one head)?

F = {HH, HT, TH} and E ∩ F = {HH}, so the answer is 1/3, not 1/2.

Rearranged: p(E ∩ F) = p(E | F) · p(F).

3Law of total probability

Split S into disjoint cases F₁, …, Fₙ that cover everything:

p(E) = p(E | F₁)p(F₁) + … + p(E | Fₙ)p(Fₙ)

Two cases: p(E) = p(E | F)p(F) + p(E | not F)p(not F).

Example · the survey trick

Flip a coin. Heads means answer “yes”; tails means answer honestly. 60% say yes.

0.6 = 1·½ + x·½, so x = p(yes | honest) = 20%.

4Bayes’ theorem

Flips a conditional around:

p(F | E) = p(E | F)p(F) / [ p(E | F)p(F) + p(E | not F)p(not F) ]

The bottom is just p(E), by the law of total probability.

Example · disease test

1% of people are sick. The test catches 90% of sick people and wrongly flags 10% of healthy people.

p(sick | positive) = (0.9·0.01) / (0.9·0.01 + 0.1·0.99) = 0.009 / 0.108 ≈ 8.3%.

Check with 1000 people: 10 are sick and 9 of them test positive; 990 are healthy and 99 of them test positive. So 9 of the 108 positives are actually sick.

Trap

p(A | B) ≠ p(B | A). A rare condition stays fairly unlikely even after a positive test.

5Independence

E, F independent ⇔ p(E ∩ F) = p(E) · p(F)

If p(F) > 0, that’s the same as p(E | F) = p(E): knowing F doesn’t change E.

Example

A random 4-bit string. E = first bit is 1; F = even number of 1s.

p(E) = ½, p(F) = ½, and p(E ∩ F) = 4/16 = ¼ = ½ · ½, so they’re independent.

Pairwise vs mutual

  • Pairwise independent: every pair satisfies the product rule.
  • Mutually independent: the product rule holds for every group of the events, including all of them at once.
  • Pairwise does not imply mutual.
Example · from lecture

Three people each pick a number from 1 to 100. A = Alice & Bob match, B = Alice & Charlotte match, C = Bob & Charlotte match.

Each event has probability 1/100, and each pair together has 1/100², so they’re pairwise independent.

But A and B together force C, so p(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 1/100² ≠ 1/100³. They’re not mutually independent.

6Bernoulli trials

n independent tries, each succeeding with probability p:

p(exactly k successes) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ
  • C(n, k) picks which tries succeed.
  • pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ is the probability of one particular pattern.
Example

A coin with p(H) = ¼ is flipped 4 times.

p(exactly 2 heads) = C(4, 2)(¼)²(¾)² = 6 · 1/16 · 9/16 = 27/128.

p(at least one head) = 1 − (¾)⁴ = 175/256. Use the complement.

7Classics from lecture

Monty Hall: switching wins with probability 2/3.

p(win) = p(win | first pick a goat)·⅔ + p(win | first pick the car)·⅓ = 1·⅔ + 0

Birthday problem: p(some two of n people share a birthday)

1 − (365 · 364 ⋯ (365 − n + 1)) / 365ⁿ

For n = 23 that’s about 50.7%. Rule of thumb: about C(n, 2)/d when picking n items from d possibilities, by the union bound over pairs.

The probabilistic method: to show something exists, build it at random and show the chance it works is above 0. Usually you do this with the union bound.

Example · from lecture

10 clubs with 5 people each. Put every student on red or blue at random.

One club is all one color with probability 2/2⁵ = 1/16. By the union bound, p(some club is one color) ≤ 10/16 < 1.

So some assignment has no club all one color.

8Counting refresher

Order mattersOrder doesn’t matter
No repetitionP(n, r) = n!/(n − r)!C(n, r)
Repetition allowednʳC(n + r − 1, r) (stars and bars)
  • Repeated letters: n!/(n₁! n₂! ⋯), e.g. LETTER = 6!/(2!·2!).
  • Product rule: multiply the counts for successive steps.
  • Sum rule: add the counts for disjoint cases.
  • Inclusion–exclusion: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|.
  • Division rule: if every outcome is counted d times, divide by d.
  • Pigeonhole: k + 1 objects in k boxes means some box gets 2. Say what the pigeons and the boxes are.
  • Binomial theorem: (x + y)ⁿ = Σ C(n, k) xⁿ⁻ᵏ yᵏ.
  • Combinatorial proof: count the same collection two ways.

9Quick traps

  1. Conditioning shrinks the denominator to p(F), not |S|.
  2. Disjoint events (each with positive probability) are not independent: if one happens, the other can’t.
  3. “At least one” → use the complement.
  4. Don’t assume independence unless the problem gives it or you check the product rule.
  5. Leave answers as C(…), powers or fractions unless told otherwise.
